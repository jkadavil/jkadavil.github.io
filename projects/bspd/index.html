Brake System Plausibility Device

A non-programmable hardware safety circuit developed for the Terps Racing Electric Vehicle to detect simultaneous braking and current conditions and trigger a timed fault response.

01 — Overview

Designed for the Terps Racing Electric Vehicle, TREV4, the Brake System Plausibility Device ensures the brake and current signals are monitored for a simultaneous over-threshold condition. The hardware detects this unsafe condition, applies defined timing behavior, and triggers the required fault response.

The implementation is entirely non-programmable: brake and current sensor voltages are compared against reference thresholds, then processed through analog, discrete logic, and timing circuitry. The circuit translates sensor thresholds and a persistence requirement into comparator and RC timing behavior.

02 — System Design

The comparators turn continuously varying sensor voltages into threshold decisions. The logic then combines the braking and throttle decisions, and the timing circuitry distinguishes a sustained conflict from a brief overlap. Keeping those functions separate made it easier to reason about what should trigger a fault and when.

The circuit resolves vehicle input signals into a single hardware fault decision through a defined comparison and timing path.

03 — Schematic Design

The threshold voltages connect the electrical circuit to the vehicle requirement. They have to represent the intended brake pressure and throttle position using the sensor signal ranges. The timing and reset paths then determine whether the output stays in its safe state, changes after a sustained conflict, or is allowed to recover.

The design uses an LM339PWR quad differential comparator containing four independent voltage comparators. These devices compare incoming vehicle signals against reference voltages to determine whether braking and current conditions exceed defined thresholds.

Because the BSPD must remain non-programmable, the logic uses analog comparators, discrete logic, passive components, and timing circuitry rather than a microcontroller.

Design considerations: Comparator threshold selection · Analog-to-digital decision boundaries · Fail-safe behavior · Timing requirements · Reset behavior · Hardware-only implementation

04 — PCB Design

The layout has to accommodate both the analog threshold circuits and the connector signals within a narrow board. The layer arrangement provides dedicated ground and power layers while keeping routing available on both outer surfaces. The schematic and layout images below show how those circuit blocks fit into the vehicle interface.

The compact 4-layer board uses a top signal layer, ground layer, power layer, and bottom signal layer. Its eight-pin connector interfaces with vehicle inputs and the BSPD fault output; some connector positions are reserved or unused in the current design.

05 — Engineering Challenges

The central challenge was translating a dense safety specification into predictable hardware behavior without programmable logic. That meant interpreting competition requirements, selecting comparator thresholds, implementing reliable timing, and designing fail-safe and reset behavior. PCB layout, iteration, hardware debugging, and system validation were all treated as part of achieving a practical implementation.

06 — What I Learned

This project strengthened my ability to translate formal requirements into hardware, design mixed-signal circuits, reason about comparator thresholds and discrete logic, iterate PCBs, debug hardware interactions, and validate safety hardware against a formal specification.

07 — Comparator Logic & Signal Integrity

The schematic uses two LM339PWR quad comparators. Brake_SIG and Curr_SIG are compared against adjustable Brake_REF and Curr_REF thresholds. Their open-collector outputs share a pull-up: any asserted comparator can pull the node low, while it rises only when both braking and current conditions exceed their references. This implements the simultaneous-condition decision without a processor.

Additional comparators monitor the sensor signals against OC_REF and SC_REF. The schematic labels these as open-circuit and short-circuit reference checks. Each input also has a 330 kΩ pull-down to define its floating state, and each comparator package has a 0.1 µF supply bypass capacitor. The sensor-range reference resistor values are unspecified in this image, so physical brake-pressure and current thresholds cannot be calculated from this revision.

08 — Nominal Fault Delay

The combined-condition node charges C3 through R9 and R11 when both input conditions are above threshold. R9 = 56 kΩ, R11 = 47 kΩ, and C3 = 4.7 µF. U2 compares the capacitor voltage with And_REF, set by a 22 kΩ upper resistor and 40 kΩ lower resistor from a 5 V supply.

Vref = 5 × 40 / (22 + 40) = 3.226 V
τ = (56 kΩ + 47 kΩ) × 4.7 µF = 0.4841 s
Vc(t) = 5 × (1 − exp(−t / τ))
ttrip = −τ × ln(1 − Vref / 5) ≈ 0.502 s

This is a nominal calculation assuming the capacitor starts discharged, a constant 5 V supply, and negligible comparator input current. Component tolerances, capacitor leakage, and initial voltage affect the actual trip time. When either condition clears, the open-collector decision node pulls low and the capacitor discharges through R11. The different charge and discharge paths explain why transient history matters.

The displayed schematic does not establish a separate latched reset or a normally-open shutdown actuator. Those vehicle-level behaviors require the corresponding interface circuitry and test evidence; they are not demonstrated by this timing calculation.
